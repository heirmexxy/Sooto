Prove a fact about money without sharing the money.

Open Financial Proof is an open format for signed answers about a person's or business's finances. One question, one verifier, one purpose. Anyone can check it. No one has to hand over their transactions.

Read the specification Verify a real proof

Every check of someone's finances collects too much and proves too little.

Lenders, landlords, universities and consulates ask for bank statements because there is nothing better. They receive PDFs they cannot verify, full of transactions they never needed.

The honest applicant gives up their privacy. The forger edits a balance and often gets through. The verifier ends up holding sensitive data that data-protection law makes them answer for.

Trust should not cost people their privacy, and checking it should not depend on spotting a forgery by eye.

  • Documents that cannot be checkedA statement PDF proves nothing about who produced it. Edited amounts and parked deposits are common in loan and visa fraud.
  • Far more data than the question needsTo learn "can this person pay ₦300,000 rent?", a landlord receives every transaction for six months.
  • Every verifier builds its own checksWithout a shared format, each lender writes its own rules, and forgers simply target the weakest.
  • People locked outTraders, farmers and freelancers with real income but scattered records cannot show it in any form a lender accepts.

How a proof moves.

        
Three parties, one signed object, no shared database.

      

      
    
        
  1. The subject asks
    A person or business asks an issuer (their bank, a wallet, an aggregator or open-source software) for a proof, naming who it is for and why.
    2. 
        
  2. The issuer signs
    The issuer computes the answer from data it can see, attaches a trust level and risk signals, and signs it. The answer is computed by fixed rules, never generated by an AI.
    3. 
        
  3. The verifier checks
    The verifier fetches the issuer's public key, runs seven checks and decides. It never sees the underlying transactions.
    4. 
      

      
    
        
  • Minimal disclosure. Yes or no, and bands. Exact figures only if the subject chooses.
    • 
        
  • One verifier, one purpose. A proof addressed to someone else is rejected.
    • 
        
  • Honest risk signals. Signs of borrowed or parked money are always included.
    • 
        
  • Deterministic claims. The same data always gives the same answer.
    • 
        
  • Short lives. Thirty days by default, ninety at most.
    • 
        
  • No data trade. Issuers may not sell or reuse the subject's data.
    • 
      

    

  


  

    

      

        
Trust you can see on every proof.

        
Verifiers set the minimum level they accept, so OFP is useful on day one with uploaded statements and grows stronger as banks join.

      

      

        
LevelSourceWhat the issuer vouches for

        
          
L1A document the subject supplied, such as a statement exportIt is internally consistent: every running balance, the totals and the dates check out.

          
L2Read live from the institution over a consented open-banking connectionThe institution reported this data at that time.

          
L3Signed by the institution itselfThe bank or wallet vouches for the answer.

Built to belong to no one.

        
OFP starts as a draft published with its reference implementation. It moves to a neutral foundation once three independent implementations exist, so no single company controls it.

      

      
    
        
  1. v0.1, nowEnvelope, canonical form, Ed25519 signatures, funds_held and cashflow templates, trust levels.
    2. 
        
  2. v0.2Proofs as W3C Verifiable Credentials and SD-JWT, presentable through OpenID4VP and the EU Digital Identity Wallet. L2 sources and L3 co-signing.
    3. 
        
  3. Test suiteSo any implementation can show it follows the specification.
    4. 
        
  4. FoundationOnce three independent implementations exist.
